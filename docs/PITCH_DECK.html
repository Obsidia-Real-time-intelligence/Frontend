Why It Is a Trust Problem
03 Why this is a trust problem, not a UX problem

Public alpha, private numbers.

01
Backtests fudge fees and slippage
Most strategies look great on paper because the math skips fills. Add real Jupiter Perps fees and slippage and the edge often disappears.
02
No proof of past calls
Telegram bots, Discord channels, X traders — all delete or omit losing calls. No one keeps an honest ledger.
03
Ranking by attention, not performance
"Top trader" usually means most retweets. There is no neutral leaderboard backed by audited fills.
04
Retail absorbs the risk
The trader copying the call eats the fees, slippage, and downside. The publisher gets the upside in followers either way.
I built a bot that returned 200% in a backtest. In production it lost 8% in two weeks. The math wasn't wrong — the assumptions were. That is the gap Obsidia is built to close. — Lopinti Abhijeeth Baba · Founder

Defensibility
08 Defensibility

A frontend is easy to copy.
The execution layer underneath is not.

M.01
Real-fee OHLCV corpus
24 months × 6 timeframes × N venues, with fee + slippage models per venue — sharpens with every new strategy a creator publishes.
M.02
Composable DSL graph
Strategies are JSON specs. Reusable, forkable, comparable side-by-side — operational know-how as code, not a black box.
M.03
Audited backtest engine
Independent re-runs on stored OHLCV produce reproducible numbers. Creator can't quietly fudge results between subscriber updates.
M.04
Closed-loop signal data
Every alert + fill + subscriber outcome feeds the marketplace ranking. Generic LLMs can copy a UI but not the labelled outcome data.
M.05
Trust + audit surfaces
Consent for data sharing, transparent fee model, public trade ledger. A retrofit for ad-hoc Telegram alpha groups; native here.
